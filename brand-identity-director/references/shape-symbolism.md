# Shape, space, and symbolic meaning

R1 §3 and R2 §3–4 propose circles/community, triangles/force, squares/structure, and grids (`REPORT-H`). These are idea prompts, not universal psychology. They do not establish causal effects, designer intent, or an audience's interpretation.

## Separate description from inference

“A circular outline encloses two elements” is a visible description. “The circle symbolizes coordination” is the proposed design rationale. “Customers feel protected” requires audience evidence. Keep all three separate. Shape meaning depends on culture, category, orientation, neighboring elements, copy, color, prior brand exposure, and practical use. A triangle can resemble a warning, play control, roof, mountain, or letter; choose based on context and test ambiguity.

For each symbolic route, document strategic attribute → proposed form → plausible alternative readings → cultural/category conflicts → recognition test. Include literal, abstract, typographic, and non-symbolic approaches when useful. Not every mark needs a concealed metaphor. If a concept needs a long explanation to be usable, inspect whether the explanation serves the designer more than the audience.

## Space is a working variable

Test positive and negative silhouettes, counters, enclosed gaps, overlap, figure/ground reversal, and crop behavior. A knockout must truly be transparent where required, not a background-colored patch that fails on a new surface. Evaluate outer clear space separately from internal spacing. Define a reproducible unit if useful, then allow documented optical corrections.

Symmetry can simplify a family, while asymmetry may suit a composition; neither implies an emotional outcome. Angles and radii can establish consistency, but there is no requirement for a golden ratio or mathematically perfect circular construction. Judge contour quality and actual-size behavior rather than decorative grid overlays.

## Example hypothesis, not a claim

For a repair cooperative, joining two open forms could suggest repair as reconnection. The same mark may read as chain links or an unrelated letter. Show it without a rationale, ask what business or action it suggests, and compare with competing marks. Reject or revise if it creates confusion. A visually distinct shape is not necessarily legally protectable.

Use shape observations in the [concept template](../templates/concepts.md). Archive failed readings and decisions so refinement does not reintroduce a rejected metaphor.
